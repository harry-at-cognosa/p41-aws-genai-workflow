# The Quiet Reorganization of the Office Library

## Background

For nearly a decade, the office library on the second floor of Hawthorne Plaza had operated under a system that no one alive had designed and no one currently employed could fully explain. Books were sorted by what longtime staff called "the colors" — a scheme in which titles were placed on shelves according to the dominant hue of their dust jackets, then sub-sorted by the typographic weight of their spine lettering. Staff who joined after 2017 generally found the system bewildering, but its proponents maintained that it produced an aesthetically calming reading room and, more importantly, that "everyone knows where everything is."

In practice, "everyone" was a small group of three: Eleanor Wisp, head librarian since 2009; Donald Marshall, her deputy; and Henrietta Cobb, a part-time volunteer who had memorized roughly 60% of the collection's locations through sheer persistence. When any of the three was unavailable, requests for specific titles were typically met with a polite delay until one of them returned.

## The Catalyst

The reorganization began, as many do, with a small failure. In late autumn, a visiting auditor from the firm's London office requested a 1987 volume on procurement law. Eleanor was on holiday in Cornwall; Donald was attending a conference in Edinburgh; and Henrietta, the only remaining keyholder of institutional memory, had been hospitalized that morning for a minor but immobilizing back complaint. The auditor, after waiting forty minutes in the reading room, was offered an apologetic refund of his coffee voucher and sent away.

The incident reached the firm's managing partner by way of an email that was not, by any reasonable reading, friendly. By the following Monday, a directive had been issued: the library would adopt a "standard cataloguing system" by the end of the quarter. The directive, characteristically, did not specify which system.

## The Working Group

A working group of seven was assembled. Its members included Eleanor (visibly displeased), Donald (cautiously diplomatic), Henrietta (still on light duty and participating by phone), and four representatives from departments that had never previously interacted with the library: Finance, IT, Communications, and an associate from the litigation team who had volunteered, it later emerged, to escape a separate committee on parking allocations.

The group's first three meetings were spent debating whether the existing color-based system constituted a "standard." Eleanor argued, with considerable passion, that any system used consistently for ten years was by definition a standard. The IT representative, a younger employee named Priya Banerjee, gently observed that the directive likely meant a system used outside the building as well as inside it. Eleanor conceded the point with visible reluctance.

By the fourth meeting, the group had narrowed the candidates to three: the Dewey Decimal Classification, the Library of Congress system, and a hybrid in-house scheme proposed by Donald that would preserve color-coded section dividers while adopting Dewey numbers for individual titles. After a vote, Donald's hybrid prevailed by four votes to three, with Eleanor abstaining.

## Execution

The physical reorganization took six weeks and was carried out on weekends by a rotating crew of paid staff and unexpectedly enthusiastic volunteers from the design team, who had become invested in the project after Priya circulated a series of before-and-after mockups. The crew encountered roughly the difficulties one would expect: misshelved volumes that had been miscatalogued for so long they were considered correct; oversized atlases that fit no proposed dimension; a cache of personal romance novels that turned out to belong to a partner who had retired in 2015.

A surprising discovery emerged in week three. Behind a row of legal reference texts, the crew found a small wooden box containing seventeen library cards from the 1970s, each issued to a person whose name no one recognized. Eleanor, when shown the box, became unusually quiet and asked to take it home. She returned it the following Monday with no comment, and the box was shelved with the firm's archival materials, where it remains.

## Outcome

The reorganized library opened in early March. Visitors are now routinely able to locate volumes without staff assistance, and the average response time for a specialty request has dropped from "approximately one to three working days" to "under five minutes." Eleanor retired in June, her departure described in the firm's internal newsletter as a transition into "well-earned rest after thirty-one years of stewardship." Donald assumed her role and has since announced a small additional reform: the color-coded section dividers, retained as a concession in the original hybrid scheme, will be replaced with plain wooden ones over the course of the next fiscal year. The change has occasioned little public comment.

## Reflection

It is tempting to read this story as a familiar parable about the inevitable triumph of standardization over idiosyncrasy. That reading is not quite wrong, but it misses something. The old system worked, after a fashion, for a long time and in the hands of a small number of people who understood it deeply. What undid it was not its inadequacy but its illegibility to anyone outside the small circle that maintained it. The new system is not better at retrieval — Eleanor could find any volume in under thirty seconds, a feat the new catalogue does not match — but it is better at being understood by people who have not spent a decade memorizing it. That is, perhaps, the underrated virtue of legibility: not that it produces the best outcomes, but that it produces outcomes that survive the absence of any particular person.
